# Theme 10 Manuscript — Science in Everyday Life

Status: **Frozen 2026.** Final theme of the platform. Full rebuild
replacing the old anecdotal Foundation reading ("The Mystery of the Wet
Can") and adding an Advanced level for the first time (previously
empty). Foundation and Advanced have deliberately different intellectual
purposes: Foundation establishes that science is not confined to
laboratories or experts — it is already behind ordinary daily life —
while Advanced asks what actually makes an approach scientific,
centering on the distinction between correlation and causation.
Foundation and Advanced Original readings were authored by the user and
are treated as authoritative; both were implemented verbatim.

---

## Educational Goal

Students understand that science is not confined to laboratories or
experts — it is a way of understanding the world that already shapes
ordinary daily life — and then examine what actually makes an approach
scientific: systematic testing, evidence, and a willingness to question
and revise conclusions, rather than simply observing that two things
occur together.

## Essential Question

Where is science already at work in everyday life, and how can we tell
whether a claim is actually supported by evidence?

## Foundation Reading — Original

**Title:** Science Is Part of Our Lives

Science is sometimes seen as something that happens in laboratories or
is studied by experts. However, science begins with something much
simpler: people observe the world, ask questions, and try to find out
how things work. Through this process, people have gained knowledge
that is now used in many parts of our everyday lives.

For example, weather forecasts are based on observations of the
atmosphere and large amounts of data. Medicine uses scientific
knowledge about the human body and diseases. The food we eat can be
kept safe for longer because people have studied how temperature and
bacteria affect it. We may not think about science when we check the
weather, take medicine, or keep food in a refrigerator, but scientific
knowledge is behind all of these things.

Science also helps people develop new technology and solve problems.
Scientists study materials, energy, plants, animals, the human body,
and many other things. What they discover can later be used by
engineers, doctors, farmers, companies, and ordinary people.

Science, then, is not separate from everyday life. It is one way people
try to understand the world through observation and investigation. What
we learn through science can then be used to improve our lives and find
new ways to deal with problems.

implemented verbatim with no changes.

## Advanced Reading — Original

**Title:** What Makes an Approach Scientific?

Science is not only a collection of knowledge. It is also a systematic
way of testing whether an explanation is supported by evidence.
Scientific investigation often begins with an observation and a
question. Researchers may then form a hypothesis and design an
experiment to test it. They collect data, compare results, and consider
whether other variables could have affected what they observed.

Suppose some students drink a certain drink before an exam and get
higher scores. We might think that the drink improved their
performance. But perhaps those students studied more, slept longer, or
were already stronger students. The drink and the higher scores may
show a correlation, but correlation alone does not prove causation. The
result might even be a coincidence. A scientific experiment therefore
tries to control other variables and make a fair comparison.

Scientists must also be careful about bias. If we already believe an
idea is correct, we may notice evidence that supports it and ignore
evidence that does not. A scientific approach requires researchers to
consider both. They also repeat experiments and ask whether other
researchers can produce consistent results. Evidence becomes more
reliable when similar results are found repeatedly under careful
conditions.

Even strong scientific conclusions can change when new evidence becomes
available. Better experiments, new data, or improved methods may show
that an earlier explanation needs to be corrected. This is not
necessarily a weakness of science. Science does not promise that every
answer is final. Its strength is that explanations can be tested,
questioned, and changed when better evidence is found.

exactly as written.

## Notice Language

### Foundation

- **Target vocabulary:** observe, expert, forecast, atmosphere, disease,
  affect, material, discover, ordinary, investigation.
- **Expressions:** "is sometimes seen as," "behind all of these
  things," "not separate from everyday life."
- **Paragraph organization:** paragraph 1 opens by naming a common
  assumption ("science... happens in laboratories or is studied by
  experts") only to immediately complicate it. The rest of the passage
  builds the case against that opening assumption before the conclusion
  states it directly.

### Advanced

- **Target vocabulary:** systematic, evidence, hypothesis, variable,
  correlation, causation, coincidence, bias, consistent, reliable.
- **Expressions:** "correlation alone does not prove causation," "even
  strong... conclusions can change," "not necessarily a weakness."
- **Reasoning language:** the passage uses hedging language ("might,"
  "perhaps," "may") throughout the drink/exam example, deliberately
  never asserting what actually caused the higher scores — modeling the
  same caution a scientific approach requires. The final paragraph uses
  a concessive structure ("This is not necessarily a weakness... Its
  strength is that...") to reframe what might sound like a flaw into the
  passage's actual argument.

## Writing

### Foundation

Status: **Revised.** The first version of this prompt ("choose one
ordinary thing you use or do almost every day... what kind of
scientific knowledge or research might be behind it?") was reviewed and
replaced with a more immediately understandable question that opens
directly onto the student's own thinking, rather than asking them to
first select an object and then speculate about a mechanism.

**Previous prompt (superseded):** Choose one ordinary thing you use or
do almost every day. What kind of scientific knowledge or research do
you think might be behind it, even if you are not sure exactly what it
is? Explain your thinking.

**Prompt:** What is something science cannot explain? Why do you think
so?

**Why this fits Foundation:** immediately understandable and fully
open — no example is suggested, so the student decides entirely for
themselves what counts and why. Requires no specialized scientific
knowledge; "why do you think so" invites personal reasoning, not a
correct technical answer. Never asks what the passage said.

**Minimum:** 3 sentences. Unchanged — the new prompt has comparable
scope.

**Sentence starters:** "I think science cannot explain..."; "This is
because..."; "Maybe someday, but for now..."

**Word bank:** expert ("Even experts don't seem to know why..."),
discover ("Maybe scientists haven't discovered enough about this
yet."), investigation ("I don't think investigation alone can explain
this."). Revised — the previous set (observe, discover, investigation)
was written for speculating about the mechanism behind a chosen object
and no longer fit; `observe` was dropped, and the `discover` and
`investigation` example phrases were rewritten to support expressing a
limit on scientific explanation rather than a mechanism behind
something. None of the three suggest a specific thing science cannot
explain.

### Advanced

Status: **Revised.** The first version of this prompt ("think of a
time when you believed one thing caused another... was that conclusion
well-supported by evidence?") was reviewed and rejected — it was too
difficult to enter, requiring students to search their memory for a
suitable cause-and-effect example and then evaluate the evidence behind
it. It also kept Writing tied to reproducing the Reading's own
correlation/causation lesson rather than moving beyond it. Revised to be
simple in language but open in thinking: a values-driven question with
no retrieval or evaluation burden.

**Previous prompt (superseded):** Think of a time when you believed one
thing caused another — in your own life, something you heard, or
something you read about. Looking back, do you think that conclusion
was well-supported by evidence? Explain your thinking.

**Prompt:** What is something you would like science to solve? Why is
it important to you?

**Why this fits Advanced:** genuinely open — no content area is
suggested in the prompt (no examples of medicine, climate, technology,
etc.), so the student decides entirely for themselves what matters and
why. It does not restate the passage's argument or its drink/exam
example, is not a two-sided or loaded choice, requires no specialized
scientific knowledge, and does not give away an answer — "why is it
important to you" has no single correct response.

**Minimum:** 4 sentences. **Recommended length:** 80–120 words.
Unchanged — the new prompt has comparable scope.

**Word bank:** evidence ("Right now, there isn't much evidence about how
to solve this."), systematic ("I think this needs more systematic
research because..."), reliable ("There isn't a reliable way to...
yet."). Revised — the previous set (evidence, correlation, coincidence)
was written for evaluating a past causal belief and no longer fit;
`correlation` and `coincidence` were dropped since they presuppose the
old retrospective-reasoning task, and `systematic` and `reliable` were
added as neutral phrases for describing a current gap in scientific
understanding, without suggesting any topic.

**Useful expressions:** "I would like science to solve..."; "This
matters to me because..."; "If this were solved,..." Revised — the
previous set ("At the time, I believed that... caused...," "Looking
back, I think...") scaffolded the old retrospective-evaluation
structure and no longer applies. The new set is topic-neutral and
matches the new prompt's want-and-why structure.

## AI Talk

### Foundation

Status: **Revised.** The first opener ("when you were younger, was
there ever something ordinary that made you wonder, 'why does that
happen?'") was reviewed and replaced with a shorter, more open question
that lets the student bring whatever association they have with
"science" at all, rather than asking specifically for a childhood
memory.

**Previous opener (superseded):** "Hi! When you were younger, was there
ever something ordinary that made you wonder, 'why does that happen?'"

**Opener:** "Hi! What does science mean to you?"

**Why this differs from Writing:** Writing asks students to name
something science cannot explain and give a reason — a focused,
opinion-with-justification task. This opener is deliberately broader
and less structured, inviting any association at all (a school subject,
a feeling, a person, nothing in particular) rather than a specific
claim and its justification. A short answer, or even "I don't know," is
a complete and usable response here.

**How the conversation might deepen:**
1. Whatever the student says, the AI can ask a natural follow-up drawn
   from their own answer, without correcting or supplementing it with
   the Reading's own definition first.
2. If the student says they don't know or gives a very short answer,
   the AI can ask something easy and concrete (e.g., what comes to mind
   first, or whether they like science class) to keep the conversation
   moving without turning it into a quiz.
3. The AI should not define science for the student before they answer,
   should not steer them toward the Reading's own framing, and should
   not turn this into a request to explain actual scientific content.

### Advanced

Status: **Revised.** The first opener ("have you ever heard someone say
'studies show...' and wondered if it was actually true?") was reviewed
and replaced so that Writing and AI Talk explore deliberately different
directions: Writing now asks what students would like science to
*solve*; AI Talk asks what students find difficult for science to
*explain*. The two are no longer variations on the same
evidence-skepticism angle.

**Previous opener (superseded):** "Hi! Have you ever heard someone say
'studies show...' and wondered if it was actually true?"

**Opener:** "Hi! Is there anything you think is difficult to explain
through science?"

**Why this differs from Writing and Mission Check:** Writing asks
students to name something they want science to solve and explain why
it matters to them — a forward-looking, values-driven task. Mission
Check tests comprehension of the correlation/causation and bias content
directly. This opener asks about the limits of scientific explanation
from the student's own perspective — a single easy, open question with
no examples supplied, genuinely easy to answer, with room to deepen
into why they think so or whether science might explain it someday,
depending on where the student's own answer leads.

**How the conversation might deepen:**
1. If the student names something, the AI can ask why they think it's
   hard to explain, without introducing the passage's vocabulary first.
2. If the conversation opens a path toward it, the AI can ask whether
   they think science might explain it someday, or whether some things
   might always stay outside science's reach — without presenting
   either view as the passage's own conclusion.
3. The AI should not turn this into a request to summarize the
   passage's argument, and should not turn the conversation into a quiz
   about the Advanced Reading.

## Takeaways

- **Foundation:** Science is already part of ordinary life — behind the
  weather forecast, the medicine we take, and the food we keep safe, is
  knowledge people gained through observation and investigation.
- **Advanced:** A scientific approach doesn't just collect knowledge —
  it tests explanations against evidence, questions its own
  conclusions, and stays open to changing them when better evidence
  appears; that openness to correction is its strength, not its
  weakness.
