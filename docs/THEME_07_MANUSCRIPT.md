# Theme 07 Manuscript — Caring for Our Planet

Status: **Frozen 2026-08-20.** Full rebuild replacing the old Aoi/beach
Foundation-only implementation with a Foundation + Advanced pair built on
the Theme 4/5/6 workflow: Foundation establishes global-scale basic
concepts, Advanced deepens one Foundation idea (understanding the problem
scientifically) into a genuinely different question (why solving it
internationally is hard) rather than restating it in harder English. This
manuscript is the authoritative source for the Theme 7 implementation.

---

## Educational Goal

Students understand that environmental problems are interconnected and
global in scale, and that even when science clearly explains what is
happening, deciding how responsibility and fairness should be shared
remains a genuinely difficult human question.

## Essential Question

Even when we understand environmental problems scientifically, why is
solving them internationally so difficult?

---

## Foundation Reading — Original

**Title:** Caring for Our Planet

**P1:** Environmental problems affect people and nature around the world.
Pollution, climate change, and damage to ecosystems may seem like separate
problems, but they are often connected. What happens in one part of the
environment can affect many other parts.

**P2:** Pollution comes from many sources. Cars and factories pollute the
air, while chemicals, waste, and plastic can enter rivers and oceans. This
pollution can damage habitats and affect the plants, animals, and people
that depend on them.

**P3:** Earth's climate has always changed. It is true that there have
been warmer and colder periods in the past. However, the problem today is
that the Earth is becoming warmer very quickly, mainly because of human
activities. Burning coal, oil, and gas releases greenhouse gases into the
atmosphere. When the climate changes quickly, plants, animals, and people
have less time to adjust. Places where we grow food or build cities may
also change. This is one reason why today's climate change is a serious
global problem.

**P4:** People depend on the environment for food, water, energy, and
other natural resources. This means that environmental problems are not
only problems for nature; they are also problems for people. Understanding
how pollution, climate, ecosystems, and human life are connected is an
important first step toward protecting our planet.

**Takeaway:** Climate has always changed naturally — but the speed of
today's warming is what makes it a serious problem.

## Advanced Reading — Original

**Title:** Caring for Our Planet

**P1:** Environmental problems do not stop at national borders. In other
words, their effects can go beyond the country where they begin. Air and
water pollution can move from one country to another, plastic waste can
travel through the oceans, and greenhouse gases released in one country
can affect the global climate. This means that many environmental problems
cannot be solved by one country alone. International cooperation is
necessary, but agreeing on what each country should do is not easy.

**P2:** One reason is that countries have not contributed equally to
today's environmental problems. Many wealthy countries used large amounts
of coal, oil, and other natural resources while developing their
economies. Other countries developed later and have contributed less in
the past. They may therefore argue that countries that contributed more to
the problem should take more responsibility for solving it. This raises a
difficult question of fairness: should every country be expected to do the
same amount?

**P3:** Economic development makes the problem even more complicated.
Developing countries need energy, transportation, factories, and jobs to
improve people's lives. Asking them to greatly reduce their use of fossil
fuels may slow their economic growth. At the same time, if every country
continues to increase its use of fossil fuels, global warming will become
more serious. Environmental protection and economic development can
therefore come into conflict, and different countries may have different
priorities.

**P4:** International agreements are one way to deal with this problem.
Countries can agree to reduce emissions, develop cleaner energy, protect
natural resources, or provide financial support to countries that need it.
However, an agreement does not guarantee that every country will reach its
goals. Governments may change, economic conditions may change, and
countries may disagree about how quickly they should act or how the costs
should be shared.

**P5:** Protecting the global environment is therefore not only a
scientific problem. Science can help us understand what is happening to
the planet, but it cannot decide how responsibility and costs should be
shared among countries. These are also economic, political, and ethical
questions. The environment is shared by everyone, but countries have
different histories, needs, and interests. Finding a solution therefore
requires not only new technology, but also cooperation and difficult
decisions about what is fair.

**Takeaway:** Science can explain what is happening to the planet, but it
cannot decide what is fair.

extreme.** Both are replaced below with words that occur naturally in the
approved text, following the instruction to resolve the two slots rather

  actual mechanism of harm the whole passage describes (pollution doesn't

**"Developing countries" — deliberately taught as a full phrase, not
"developing" alone.** The Japanese gloss explicitly states that these are
countries whose economies and societies are in the process of developing,
many of which are experiencing significant economic growth — not poor,
backward, or inferior countries. This matters directly for the passage's
own argument: the tension in paragraph 3 only makes sense if developing
countries' growth needs are taken seriously, not dismissed as a lesser
concern.

**`conflict` and `guarantee`** appear naturally in the Advanced reading
("come into conflict," "does not guarantee") but are deliberately **not**
among the ten target words — both are already taught elsewhere (`lt-s-05`
reinforced as `pw-s-01`; `pw-s-10`), and using them again here was judged
unnecessary duplication rather than useful reinforcement.

**Spiral reinforcement — `responsibility` and `priority`.** Both were
previously taught in Theme 2 in a personal context: `responsibility`
(`us-f-05`, Foundation) about managing one's own coursework and
independence; `priority` (`us-s-06`, Advanced) about a student's own
time-management choices. Theme 7 extends both to a national/international
scale — which countries bear how much responsibility for a shared global
problem, and what a whole country chooses to prioritize when environmental
and economic goals compete.

## Notice Language

### Foundation

- **Target vocabulary:** environment, damage, ecosystem, climate,
  pollution, resource, atmosphere, greenhouse gas, habitat, adjust.
- **Expressions:** "may seem like separate problems, but...," "less time
  to adjust."
- **Tone/purpose:** The passage makes a careful, deliberate distinction —
  Earth's climate has always changed naturally, and that fact does not
  make today's rapid warming less serious. The concern is the *speed* of
  change, not the mere fact that change is happening. This is stated
  directly in paragraph 3 rather than left for students to infer.

### Advanced

- **Target vocabulary:** contribute, wealthy, developing countries, fossil
  fuel, emissions, fairness, responsibility, priority, ethical, border.
- **Expressions:** "do not stop at national borders," "come into
  conflict," "not only a scientific problem."
- **Tone/purpose:** The passage presents the historical-responsibility
  argument (paragraph 2) and developing countries' growth needs
  (paragraph 3) with equal sympathy, framing both as legitimate positions
  rather than declaring one side right — the same even-handed treatment
  Theme 6 used for competing wartime narratives.

## Mission Check

### Foundation

**Understanding:** According to the passage, what is the real concern
about today's climate change?
- A. That the climate is changing at all, which has never happened
  before. *(wrong)*
- B. That the Earth is warming much faster than in the past, giving less
  time to adjust. *(correct)*
- C. That scientists cannot agree on whether the climate is changing.
  *(wrong)*
- Answer paragraph: P3.

**Evidence:** Which sentence best shows that environmental problems are
not only problems for nature, but also for people?
- P1: "Pollution, climate change, and damage to ecosystems may seem like
  separate problems, but they are often connected." *(wrong — about
  interconnection, not people)*
- P2: "This pollution can damage habitats and affect the plants, animals,
  and people that depend on them." *(wrong — plausible, but framed via
  habitat damage, not direct dependency)*
- P4: "People depend on the environment for food, water, energy, and
  other natural resources. This means that environmental problems are not
  only problems for nature; they are also problems for people."
  *(correct)*

**Vocabulary in Context:** In "plants, animals, and people have less time
to adjust," what does "adjust" mean?
- A. To completely stop changing. *(wrong)*
- B. To change gradually in response to new conditions. *(correct)*
- C. To move to a new location. *(wrong)*

### Advanced

**Understanding:** According to the passage, why do some people argue that
wealthy countries should take on more responsibility for solving
environmental problems?
- A. Because wealthy countries have more money to spend on anything.
  *(wrong)*
- B. Because wealthy countries contributed more to the problem while
  developing their economies in the past. *(correct)*
- C. Because wealthy countries have more scientists studying the
  environment. *(wrong)*
- Answer paragraph: P2.

**Evidence:** Which sentence best shows that solving this problem is not
simply a matter of every country reducing fossil fuel use equally?
- P1: "International cooperation is necessary, but agreeing on what each
  country should do is not easy." *(wrong — too general)*
- P3: "Developing countries need energy, transportation, factories, and
  jobs to improve people's lives. Asking them to greatly reduce their use
  of fossil fuels may slow their economic growth." *(correct)*
- P4: "Governments may change, economic conditions may change, and
  countries may disagree about how quickly they should act or how the
  costs should be shared." *(wrong — about agreement fragility, not
  equal-reduction complexity)*

**Interpretation:** The passage says "Science can help us understand what
is happening to the planet, but it cannot decide how responsibility and
costs should be shared among countries." What is this sentence suggesting
about the limits of science in solving this problem?
- A. Science is not useful for understanding environmental problems at
  all. *(wrong)*
- B. Science can explain what is happening, but deciding what is fair is
  a different kind of question. *(correct)*
- C. Science and fairness are actually the same thing. *(wrong)*

This Advanced sequence directly tests the passage's deepest claim — the
distinction between what science can and cannot decide — not recall of a
single fact.

## Writing

### Foundation

**Prompt:** Have you noticed any environmental changes in a place you know
well? What is one thing you think people — including you — can do about
environmental problems?

**Why this fits Foundation:** it connects the reading's global-scale
content to the student's own local observation and agency, without asking
them to reproduce anything from the passage. Every student can answer this
regardless of scientific background.

**Minimum:** 3 sentences.

**Sentence starters:** "I have noticed..."; "One thing I think people can
do is..."; "I already try to..."

**Word bank:** environment ("It's important to protect the environment
by..."), pollution ("I noticed pollution when..."), resource ("We can use
resources like water more carefully."), damage ("Even small actions can
help reduce damage to nature.").

### Advanced

Status: **Revised 2026-08-21.** The original two-sided prompt ("Some
people believe individuals should change their own daily habits... Other
people believe that only governments and large companies can make a real
difference...") was reviewed and found to be rhetorically imbalanced: the
second option ("only... a real difference") was phrased far more forcefully
than the first ("should change... to help"), which risked quietly steering
students toward it as the more "sophisticated" position rather than
letting them arrive at one on equal footing. The underlying task —
individual vs. institutional responsibility, position + reasons — was
sound and is preserved; only the framing changed, to a neutral open
question across three options instead of two asymmetrically-weighted ones.

**Prompt:** Who should do more to solve environmental problems:
individuals, governments, or companies? What do you think? Give reasons
for your opinion.

**Why this fits Advanced:** it preserves the same content-based
university-study direction as before — a genuine, widely-debated
real-world issue, distinct from the reading's specific argument about
wealthy/developing countries, requiring a real position with reasons
(real issue → position → reasons → explanation), with no specialist
political knowledge needed. All three options are named with identical,
neutral phrasing ("individuals, governments, or companies"), and the
prompt supplies no argument or hint toward any of them. It still does not
ask "what did the reading say."

**Minimum:** 4 sentences. **Recommended length:** 80–120 words.

**Word bank:** responsibility ("I think governments have more
responsibility than individuals because..."), priority ("This should be a
bigger priority for..."), ethical ("This is also an ethical question,
because...") — chosen because they support constructing a position on any
of the three options without supplying the argument itself. Unchanged
from the previous version — still generic enough to fit the revised
prompt.

**Useful expressions:** "In my opinion,..."; "One reason I think this
is..."; "This is important because..."

## AI Talk

### Foundation

**Opener:** "Hi! Do you ever think about where your trash goes after you
throw it away?"

**Why this differs from Writing:** Writing asks about noticed change and
possible action; this opener is pure curiosity about an everyday,
overlooked process — a fresh, easy entry point, not a spoken repeat of the
Writing prompt.

**How the conversation might deepen:**
1. If the student wonders where trash actually goes, the AI can ask what
   they think happens to it, gently introducing ideas like landfills or
   recycling without lecturing.
2. If the conversation touches on feeling like one person can't make a
   difference, the AI can gently connect that to the idea that individual
   actions add up, without pushing a specific action.
3. The AI should not steer toward international or political territory
   (wealthy vs. developing countries, agreements) — that belongs to the
   Advanced conversation, not this one.

### Advanced

**Opener:** "Hi! Have you ever disagreed with someone about how serious a
problem is, even though you both agreed on the basic facts?"

**Why this differs from Writing and Mission Check:** Writing asks for a
position on individual vs. government responsibility; Mission Check tests
comprehension of the wealthy/developing-countries argument and the
science/fairness distinction. This opener is personal and generic — no
environmental content is needed to answer it — and only *may* lead toward
the reading's deepest idea (agreeing on facts doesn't mean agreeing on
values or priorities) if the conversation naturally goes there.

**How the conversation might deepen:**
1. If the student describes such a disagreement, the AI can ask what made
   it hard to resolve even though the facts were shared — gently
   connecting to the reading's point that science can inform but not
   settle questions of fairness or priority.
2. If the conversation turns to real environmental examples, the AI can
   ask who the student thinks should decide what's fair when countries
   disagree, without asking the student to pick a "correct" side.
3. The AI should avoid turning this into a debate about which countries
   are right — the reading's point is that this is a genuinely open
   question, not one with a defensible correct answer.
