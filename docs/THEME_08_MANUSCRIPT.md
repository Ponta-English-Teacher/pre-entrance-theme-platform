# Theme 08 Manuscript — Living with AI

Status: **Frozen 2026-08-21.** Full rebuild replacing the old Kenta
essay-writing anecdote with a Foundation (history: what happened) +
Advanced (future: what may happen next) pair, built to remain
educationally relevant regardless of which specific AI product or model
is current when a student reads it. Foundation was substantially revised
2026-08-27 for a plainer, less detailed passage. Advanced was
substantially revised 2026-08-21 to center on the division of work
between people and AI and the moving boundary between human and machine
work, deliberately de-emphasizing responsibility as an organizing topic.
Historical claims verified against external sources (see § Factual

---

## Educational Goal

Students understand that generative AI's rapid public adoption after 2022
was a shift in access and use, not the invention of AI itself — and that
as AI becomes capable of a wider range of work, the division of work
between people and machines may continue to shift, raising genuine,
still-unresolved questions about human ability, work, and control that
will outlast any single technology.

## Essential Question

As AI becomes capable of a wider range of work, how might the division of
work between people and machines change — and why does this create both
hope and fear?

**Title:** Artificial Intelligence

**P1:** Artificial intelligence, or AI, did not begin with ChatGPT. AI had
already been used for many years in computers, machines, and online
services, usually working behind them without people communicating
directly with it. Most people therefore used AI without thinking much
about the AI itself.

**P2:** This began to change quickly when OpenAI released ChatGPT to the
public in late 2022. It was a chatbot that could use what it had learned
from a huge amount of data to respond to people's questions and produce
new content. For the first time, millions of ordinary people began
communicating directly with this kind of AI simply by typing what they
wanted to know or do.

**P3:** Since then, AI has become much more involved in everyday life.
People can ask AI to explain something they do not understand, help them
write or improve a text, translate languages, find and organize
information, create images, or help solve problems. At work, AI can also
help people analyze information, prepare documents, and develop ideas. The
important change is that ordinary people can now ask AI for help directly
and use it for many different purposes.

**P4:** The rapid spread of AI also changed how organizations used
technology. Companies began adding AI to their products and services, and
schools and workplaces began finding ways to use it. AI became something
that more people could use in their daily activities, rather than
something that mainly worked behind other technology. In only a few
years, the relationship between people and AI changed greatly.

**Takeaway:** In only a few years, the relationship between people and AI
changed greatly.

**Title:** Living with AI

**P1:** Since ChatGPT made direct interaction with AI widely available, AI
has continued to develop quickly. It is becoming able to perform a wider
range of tasks once thought to need human knowledge, judgment, or
skill — moving beyond repetitive and systematic work toward analysis,
planning, some management, and even intellectual or creative work. This
does not mean AI can do these jobs alone, only that it is becoming part
of work that used to depend entirely on people.

**P2:** One common prediction is that AI will replace many jobs. A more
important change, though, may be that the division of work between
people and AI shifts. AI may take on more parts of a task while people
focus on different parts — asking the right questions, judging results,
or handling situations AI cannot manage. Some jobs may disappear, many
may change, and new kinds of work may appear, including in professional
and managerial roles.

**P3:** AI may make work faster and help people process more information,
freeing them to focus on harder problems. But if people regularly let AI
perform certain tasks, they may practice those abilities less themselves.
This raises a real question about which human abilities remain important
as AI produces more answers on its own.

**P4:** These changes also create fear. One is the more immediate fear of
losing jobs, roles, or skills as AI takes on more of what people used to
do. A more serious concern, raised by some experts, is that future AI
could become capable enough that people find it difficult to understand
or control — a real concern, not an established fact. Both point to the
same underlying shift: the boundary between human work and machine work
keeps moving, and as it does, work, education, and the value of human
skill may change with it.

**Takeaway:** The boundary between human work and machine work keeps
moving — and as it does, work, education, and the value of human skill
may change with it.

## Advanced Reading — Simplified

**Important:** simplified language, not simplified thinking or
simplified certainty. Every hedge ("may," "could," "not an established
fact") is preserved exactly — none are strengthened into "will."

**P1:** Since ChatGPT made it easy for people to talk directly with AI,
AI has kept developing quickly. It is becoming able to do a wider range
of tasks — tasks that used to need human knowledge, judgment, or skill.
This includes not only repetitive and systematic work, but also analysis,
planning, some management, and even intellectual or creative work. This
doesn't mean AI can do these jobs completely by itself. It means AI is
becoming part of work that used to depend entirely on people.

**P2:** One common prediction is that AI will replace many jobs. But a
more important change may be this: the division of work between people
and AI may shift. AI may do more parts of a task, while people focus on
other parts — asking the right questions, judging the results, or
handling situations AI cannot manage. Some jobs may disappear. Many jobs
may change. And new kinds of work may appear, including in professional
and managerial jobs.

**P3:** AI may make work faster. It may help people process more
information, so they can focus on harder problems. But if people
regularly let AI do certain tasks, they may practice those same abilities
less themselves. This raises a real question: which human abilities will
still be important, as AI produces more answers by itself?

**P4:** These changes also create fear. One fear is more immediate:
people may lose jobs, roles, or skills as AI does more of what people
used to do. A more serious concern, raised by some experts, is that
future AI could become capable enough that people find it hard to
understand or control. This is a real concern — but it is not an
established fact. Both fears point to the same deeper change: the
boundary between human work and machine work keeps moving. As it moves,
work, education, and the value of human skill may change too.

## Writing

### Foundation

**Prompt:** Do you use AI tools in your own life, such as for schoolwork
or daily tasks? What do you find helpful about using AI, and what makes
you want to check its answers carefully?

**Why this fits Foundation:** personal and immediately answerable
regardless of how much or little AI a student actually uses — "I don't
use it much" is itself a valid, usable answer. Never asks what the
reading said.

**Minimum:** 3 sentences.

**Sentence starters:** "I use AI to..."; "I find it helpful when...";
"I try to check AI's answers by..."

**Word bank:** AI ("I use AI to..."), chatbot ("I use a chatbot to..."),
respond ("AI can respond quickly, but..."), translate ("I sometimes use
AI to translate..."). Updated 2026-08-27 — the two entries previously
tied to `access` and `task` were swapped to `chatbot` and `translate`
after those ids were repurposed to different words; the prompt itself is
unchanged (still factually and thematically compatible with the revised
passage).

### Advanced

Status: **Revised again.** The second version of this prompt ("think
about a job you know... what parts of this job might AI do, and what
parts might people still do") was reviewed and rejected — it requires
high school students to understand the internal tasks of a specific job
and predict which parts AI may take over, which needs workplace
knowledge and experience many students do not yet have. An alternative
(asking what students would personally like AI to help them with) was
also considered and rejected, since it still requires imagining specific
future AI uses. Revised to be deliberately broader: students may think
about work, education, communication, creativity, daily life, human
ability, or anything else they judge important, but the prompt itself
names none of these — the student decides what matters.

**Previous prompt (superseded, job-based version):** Think about a job
you know. In the future, what parts of this job might AI do, and what
parts might people still do? Explain why.

**Earlier superseded prompt:** As AI takes on more parts of different
kinds of work, which job or kind of work do you think will change the
most in the next ten years? Explain what you think will change, and why.

**Prompt:** How do you think AI will change human life in the future?
What changes do you think will be important? Explain your ideas.

**Why this fits Advanced:** genuinely open — the student supplies both
the topic area and the reasoning, with no example domains named in the
prompt and no requirement to reason about job tasks or predict specific
AI capabilities. It does not restate "what did the reading say," is not
a two-sided or advantages/disadvantages structure, and does not steer
the student toward the reading's own conclusion (the moving boundary
between human and machine work) — a student is free to write about
something the reading never mentions at all.

**Minimum:** 4 sentences. **Recommended length:** 80–120 words. Unchanged
from the previous version — the new prompt has comparable scope.

**Word bank:** prediction ("My prediction is that..."), capable ("As AI
becomes more capable, I think..."), underlying ("I think the underlying
reason this matters is..."). Revised — the previous set (division,
prediction, capable) leaned on job/task-of-work phrasing that no longer
fits; `division` was dropped since "division of work" presupposes the
job framing this revision removes, and `underlying` was added as a
neutral, content-free sentence frame. All three example phrases were
also rewritten to remove any reference to "this job."

**Useful expressions:** "I think AI will change..."; "This change is
important because..."; "In the future, I think..." Revised — the
previous set ("AI might be able to...," "But people might still need
to...") explicitly scaffolded an AI-does-X/people-do-Y structure, which
conflicts with the new prompt's deliberately open framing. The new set
is topic-neutral and does not suggest any content area.

## AI Talk

### Foundation

**Opener:** "Hi! Can you remember the first time you used an AI tool,
like ChatGPT or something similar? What was that like?"

**Why this differs from Writing:** Writing asks for a general reflective
statement (what's helpful, what to check); this opener asks for a
specific first-time memory — a narrative recall task, not a repeat of the
same reflective question in spoken form.

**How the conversation might deepen:**
1. If the student describes a specific memory, the AI can ask what
   surprised them most about it, without steering toward any particular
   reaction.
2. If the conversation touches on trusting or not trusting what AI
   produces, the AI can gently connect that to the idea that AI's answers
   are worth checking, without lecturing.
3. The AI should not steer toward future-of-work or responsibility
   territory — that belongs to the Advanced conversation, not this one.

### Advanced

Status: **Rebuilt 2026-08-21** for the final passage. No longer connects
to responsibility (removed from the reading); now ties to the passage's
hope/fear framing in P4 while staying fully personal and generic.

**Opener:** "Hi! When you imagine AI in twenty years, do you feel
curious, worried, or a mix of both? What makes you feel that way?"

**Why this differs from Writing and Mission Check:** Writing asks
students to predict which specific job or kind of work will change most;
Mission Check tests comprehension of the division-of-work and
concern-vs-fact content. This opener asks for a personal, immediate gut
reaction — no environmental or work-specific content is needed to
answer — with room to deepen into any of the passage's specific areas
(jobs, abilities, control) depending on where the student's own answer
leads.

**How the conversation might deepen:**
1. If the student says worried, the AI can ask what specifically worries
   them — losing a skill, a job changing, something else — and let the
   student's own answer guide which area the conversation explores.
2. If the student says curious, the AI can ask what kind of work they
   think might change in a way they'd find interesting rather than
   worrying, connecting naturally to the passage's division-of-work idea.
3. The AI should avoid presenting the loss-of-control concern as a
   confirmed prediction or settled fact — matching the reading's own
   careful distinction between concern and established fact.

## Factual Sources for Historical Claims

- ChatGPT was released to the public by OpenAI on November 30, 2022, as a
  free research preview, requiring no special technical access. ([History.com](https://www.history.com/this-day-in-history/november-30/chatgpt-released-openai); [Wikipedia: ChatGPT](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ChatGPT))
- ChatGPT reached over 100 million users within approximately two months
  of release, reported as the fastest user growth of a consumer
  application at that time. (Corroborated across multiple sources
  including [Yahoo Finance](https://finance.yahoo.com/news/chatgpt-was-unveiled-3-years-ago-kicking-off-the-ai-revolution-for-investors-it-did-even-more-110014912.html) and [ofzenandcomputing.com](https://www.ofzenandcomputing.com/chatgpt-release-date/))
- AI as a research field is conventionally dated to the 1956 Dartmouth
  Summer Research Project, where the term "artificial intelligence" was
  coined by John McCarthy; Alan Turing's 1950 paper "Computing Machinery
  and Intelligence" is an earlier foundational reference point.
  ([Dartmouth](https://home.dartmouth.edu/about/artificial-intelligence-ai-coined-dartmouth); [Wikipedia: Dartmouth workshop](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dartmouth_workshop))
- ChatGPT was **not** the first generative AI or the first AI ordinary
  people could use — GPT-3 was accessible via API (with a waitlist, then
  fully open by November 2021) before ChatGPT existed, and consumer AI
  products such as voice assistants (e.g., released from the early 2010s
  onward) and machine translation tools were already in wide use for
  years beforehand. This is why the manuscript deliberately avoids
  "ChatGPT was the first AI ordinary people could use" and instead frames
  the change as ChatGPT making generative AI directly and easily
  accessible through a simple conversational interface, without technical
  or developer access, at unprecedented speed and scale. ([GPT-3 Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/GPT-3); reporting on GPT-3 API general availability, November 2021)
